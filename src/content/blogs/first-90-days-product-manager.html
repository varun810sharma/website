My product role at PMI isn't a typical one. I'm not shipping to end consumers. I'm building tools for ground sales teams who then go out and sell to customers, which makes it B2B2C. Common in retail, but it means my customer is two people at once: the rep in the field and the shopper they're trying to convert. Most PM frameworks don't account for that. I figured it out early, and it changed how I approached everything.

That experience is what this piece is about. Not a generic 90-day playbook. A set of things I wish someone had told me before I started.

Every new PM role starts the same way. You show up, get the Teams or Slack access, meet the team, and immediately feel like you're three months behind on everything. That feeling is normal. It's also a trap.

The instinct is to move fast, show impact, prove you belong. Resist it.

Find the people who'll tell you the truth

The first thing I do in any new role is identify two or three people willing to teach me how the organization actually works, not how the org chart says it does. Every company has its own immune system. What's tolerated, what's sacred, what'll get you ignored for six months if you do it wrong. You can't read that in a deck or a strategy paper. Find the mentors early, ask dumb questions, and listen more than you talk.

Build relationships before you build anything else

Go deep with the teams you work with, whether that's engineering, design, or something else entirely. If your product touches customers directly, go deep with customer success. Then go one level further: get in front of the actual end user. Everything should flow backward from there. The question I make a point of asking everyone is what they wish the product did differently, what they think has worked, and where they'd take it in two years. You learn more in those conversations than in a month of Jira tickets.

Study the graveyard

Every product team has one. Features that got killed, bets that didn't ship, initiatives that died in committee, priorities that got backlogged indefinitely. Most new PMs never look at it. That's a mistake. The graveyard tells you more about the org's real constraints, political fault lines, and what the market actually rejected than any current roadmap will. Ask what got cut in the last two years and why. The answers are rarely "it wasn't a good idea."

Find the longest-tenured skeptic

Every team has someone who's been around long enough to watch three or four product owners cycle through with big plans and mixed results. Most people treat them as a blocker. They're actually a map. They carry institutional memory that no onboarding doc has. Buy them coffee. Ask what they've seen fail. They'll tell you things no one else will, because they've already decided they have nothing to prove.

Don't touch the vision. Not yet.

This is the one people get wrong most. A product you've inherited was built by people who understood it in ways you don't yet. Don't gut their logic in week three. The authority to change direction has to be earned and agreed on. Changing course without that buy-in doesn't make you visionary; it makes you the person who blew up something that was quietly working.

Be useful before you're strategic

In the first few months, your job is to unblock people. Help the teams you work with make tradeoff calls. Help engineering or IT prioritize. The framework I keep coming back to: if it reduces costs or drives revenue, fix it first, prove it with an A/B test, then scale. Not every ecosystem lets you run a clean test, so sometimes it's a focused pilot. Either way, understand the full customer journey before you touch anything. Changes that look clean in isolation can break things downstream.

Say no to something small, early

The instinct in a new role is to say yes to everything. Build goodwill, avoid conflict, keep everyone happy. But establishing early that you can hold a line, even on something minor, signals you have a point of view. People follow PMs who have one. Endless accommodation reads as someone who doesn't know what they think yet.

Use the product like a user

This sounds obvious. Most PMs skip it anyway. Sit down, open the product, and actually use it the way a customer would. Watch real users do the same. Nine times out of 10, what looks like a product problem is a people problem: a workflow, a missing explanation, a friction point nobody thought to name.

Not everything needs AI

If you work on a product used by real people, start with those people. Bring in AI use cases when they solve a specific, observed problem. Not because it's a good story for the roadmap. People first, technology second.

The 90 days pass faster than you expect. The PMs who land well aren't the ones who moved fastest. They're the ones who listened longest and built enough trust that people let them move at all.
