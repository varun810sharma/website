I started lifting in 2017, and for most of the seven years after that, the gym was my entire definition of fitness. On and off, never for competition, but often enough to build a habit I trusted. It carried me through a few cycles of fat loss where training was the one part of the plan I never had to think about, and it gave me a base of strength and, honestly, a confidence I didn't have before. You judged the work by what the gym did to your shape and how you felt walking out, and that was the scoreboard. Fitness mattered to me, but it was one priority among several. Somewhere in the last two years that order flipped. Health now sits above everything else I do. I usually train before sunrise because that's the one slot nothing else can take. When I travel, I plan around where I can run. None of that was true before the summer of 2025.

The shift has a date. In July last year I did my first Hyrox in Abu Dhabi, and I trained for it with my friend Sahitya. We ran intervals I'd never done, practiced stations I'd rarely touched, and did simulations (two of them) to find out where we'd break. Both times, the answer was the same. The stations felt manageable. The running wrecked us, with our heart rates spiking within minutes, because our legs weren't used to moving like that. For anyone who hasn't heard of Hyrox, the race is eight kilometers of running cut into one kilometer pieces, with a station after each (ski erg, sled push, sled pull, burpee broad jumps, row, farmers carry, lunges, wall balls). We finished, and I walked out with a muscle pull and something the mirror hadn't told me in seven years. I was carrying muscle I had little use for on top of an aerobic base I'd never built.

So I did the right thing to do right after - joined a run club. Uncommon Club, nearly every Thursday morning at Kite Beach, 5:15AM, for a year now. I hit my goal pace at my first official half marathon in about 6 months by February 2026, and I'm now training for my first Ironman 70.3 in December 2026 and my first marathon in January 2027. The training takes 12 to 14 hours a week and leaves little time for the gym. I'm smaller than I was in 2024, and my aerobic fitness is the best it's ever been. I traded size for an engine, and I was nowhere near the first. Millions of people made some version of the same trade over the same time, and capitalism followed them.

There are two stories I read about what's happening in fitness right now, and I don't trust either. The first is that gym culture is finished, replaced by run clubs, Pilates and whatever Hyrox is. The second is that this is another trend cycle, the same one that gave us CrossFit and SoulCycle, and it'll burn out the same way. In my opinion, neither holds true in silos. The gym is still growing as a business. What it's losing is the right to decide what being fit means, and that definition has moved somewhere the gym never learned to go: toward community, and toward a finish line. Each of those has been tried individually but now what's working is both of them together. The fitness related ideas winning now sell a version of the both combined.

A Race Built From Gym Equipment Is Now Priced Like Ironman

Hyrox started in Hamburg with 650 people. Last season, ~1.5 million raced it. On 8 September 2026 its founders, Christian Toetzke and Moritz Fürste, bought back control in a deal reported at ~$700M. That's roughly what Advance paid for Ironman in 2020 ($730M), a brand that has sold finish lines since 1978 and basically invented the endurance category. The comparison isn't fair though. Wanda sold Ironman under pressure in the first weeks of COVID, and the Hyrox figure is reported rather than filed. Discount both as much as you like and the gap has still closed in under a decade.

The more interesting question is what people are paying for. Think about what Hyrox sells. An experience with sled, a rower, a ski erg and a pile of wall balls. Every decent gym has had that kit for years and rents it to you by the month via subscription. Hyrox puts the same kit in a hall for one morning, and people cross borders to use it. Sounds odd, until you notice the equipment wasn’t the product. People pay for what surrounds it: other people on the floor with them, and a finish line with their time next to it. The gym had the equipment all along and never sold either of those, and that gap is the story of how this category evolved.

The Gym Is Dying as the Place Fitness Happens

Quick clarification, because the gym still makes money, and plenty of it. Planet Fitness, a leader in the mass gym category, did $1.3B of revenue last year from 20.8 million members, and global gym memberships grew 6% in 2024. Weight training was the fastest growing activity among women on Strava that year. People haven't stopped lifting. They've stopped treating the gym as the point. The gym is dying as the place that defines fitness, and the reason sits in how the business is built.

The gym sells access and then leaves you alone with it. Your result has no effect on its revenue. The gym that gets you fit and the gym that lets you drift bill the same monthly fee, so neither has much reason to give you a goal. The only scoreboard on offer is the mirror, which moves slowly and rewards one kind of body. And nobody in the room knows your name. You can train somewhere four times a week for a year and leave without a single friend. The model works as a utility, but it can’t give you a reason to show up beyond the fee you’ve already paid and the discipline you’ve built yourself. None of this is a case against lifting. Strength still belongs in any serious plan, and it carries over into everything else you do. It works best as one part of a wider routine, not the whole definition of fit.

The cheap end survives that, because cheap access is a utility, like a mobile data plan. The pressure lands on premium rooms, and SoulCycle is the one case worth studying, because it had the thing the gym never did. It had people. For a few years it was the closest thing fitness had to a cult. It grew to more than 60 studios, closed 20 in 2022, and shut at least six more on a single day in June this year, and its owner is Equinox, which understands premium fitness as well as anyone. The community kept the room full while it was new. What SoulCycle never had was a finish line. Every class ended the same way, and nothing you did in week 40 counted toward anything, so once Peloton put the same class in your living room, there was little reason left to come in. People without a finish line fade. A finish line without people is a gym with a mirror.

Pilates studios are next, for the same reason. Pilates has been the most booked workout on ClassPass for three years running, with bookings up 66% in 2025, so the activity will keep growing. The buildout won't hold. Reformer rooms carry SoulCycle's exact math: heavy capex, a fixed number of attendees per class, rent in the neighborhoods where rent hurts most, and nothing stopping the studio next door from copying it. Expect visible closures in oversupplied cities within two to three years while participation keeps climbing.

Run Clubs Are a Loneliness Story Before They're a Fitness Story

A gym is a room full of people in headphones avoiding eye contact. A run club is built the other way around. Fixed time, fixed place, pace groups, and people who notice when you skip. That difference sounds small until you look at what people in their 20s and 30s are dealing with. Gallup's survey across more than 140 countries found about a quarter of adults feel lonely, and the rate is highest among 19 to 29 year olds, not the elderly. In separate US polling, 25% of men aged 15 to 34 said they felt lonely a lot of the previous day, against 18% of adults overall. At the same time, the rooms where people that age used to meet each other are closing.

Run clubs filled that gap. Strava counted a 59% rise in running club participation in 2024. In a survey of 5,068 people, 58% said they'd made new friends through fitness groups, rising to 66% among Gen Z. Gen Z were four times more likely to want to meet people while working out than at a bar, and one in five had dated someone they met exercising. Notice that none of those numbers are about fitness. They're about friendship, dating and belonging, and the run happens to be the venue.

Every Run Club Points You at a Finish Line

Nobody at a run club asks what you bench. They ask what you're training for, and there's always an answer, and there's always a next one. The standard path is well worn: a first 5K, then a 10K, a half marathon, a marathon, and an ultra. Mine went sideways into triathlon, which splits the same effort across swimming, cycling and running and asks you to be decent (read as average) at all three at once. The paths look different and end the same way, with a medal and a time. Each step is hard enough to change your week and close enough to believe you can do it. That combination is what brings people back on Thursday mornings long after the novelty of the group wears off.

The finish time is also a better credential than the body ever was. A big physique was a claim about discipline and hours spent, but it was vague, it favored a narrow type, and it said as much about genetics as effort. A finish time makes the same claim with a number attached. It's public, dated and comparable. Strava found Gen Z are 75% more likely than Gen X to train for a specific race or event. Training has a deliverable now, and the deliverable is something you can put in your Instagram bio.

Here's the catch. Credentials inflate. When 650 people finished Hyrox in Hamburg, finishing meant something. At 1.5 million a year, finishing is closer to attendance, and the people who already own the shirt go looking for the next rung. I've been climbing that ladder myself for about 14 months: Hyrox, a run club, a half marathon, and next the 70.3 and the full marathon. The last rung never got boring. It stopped saying anything, and by then the club was already pointing at the next one. That ladder is the product, and the company that understood it best went one step further and built its ladder inside the gym.

What Comes After the Finish Line

Step back and the pattern is clear. The mirror was the first definition of fitness. The finish line is the second. The third is already forming, and it's years. This is where my own story started, and income is what spreads it. When people earn more, health stops being one priority among several, which is the same shift I went through. McKinsey's 2025 survey found up to 60% of consumers across major markets rate healthy aging as a top or very important priority, and Gen Z and millennials are 36% of adults but 41% of the spending in a ~$2T wellness market. People spend in a predictable order: diagnostics first, then coaching, then sleep and recovery, then a clinic membership.

Equinox already sells a program called Optimize for $40,000 a year, with lab work across about 100 biomarkers twice a year, and more than 1,000 people are on the waitlist. That's more than 200 years of a basic Planet Fitness membership, paid annually, with a queue. Longevity clinics keep opening across Dubai for people who want the same product without the flight to New York. My narrowest prediction is this. Longevity gets sold the way races are sold now: a baseline, a target, a retest date and a group doing it with you. The blood test becomes the next finish line. Expect the word longevity on gym pricing pages long before the evidence behind it catches up, and expect the operators who wrap it in community to beat the ones who sell it as a private service.

The bigger shift is in how people get reached. The gym model never reached most of them. Only 32% of Americans hit 150 minutes of activity a week, after four decades of gyms on every corner. What reaches people now is a date and a crowd. Strava counted club organized events up 1.5 times in 2025, on top of a near fourfold jump in the number of clubs. The future of the fitness category is community led activations and events, and the brands that want this customer will spend less telling them what to buy and more hosting the morning they show up.

The gym sold a room. The run club sells a morning, and the race sells a reason to come back to it. The equipment barely changed in 40 years. The reason to show up did, and the businesses that understand that are the ones building the next decade of this category.

I'm weaker than I was two years ago and I can cycle for five hours straight. Only one of those used to count in 2017.
