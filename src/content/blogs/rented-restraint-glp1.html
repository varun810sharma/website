The glass of milk that sat next to the American breakfast plate for most of the last century began as an inventory problem. Farmers had more milk than the country was drinking, and instead of producing less of it, the industry set out to grow the appetite until it matched the supply. That's worth remembering right now, because we've just built the first tool that runs the process the other way, and almost nobody in consumer goods has priced in what that means. This piece is about that tool. It starts with milk because milk is the clearest proof that the hunger you think of as yours was often someone else's idea first.

Milk was a campaign before it was a habit.

The American Dairy Association came together in 1940, built by farmers and cooperatives sitting on a surplus that needed somewhere to go. Their answer was demand-creation at national scale. By the early 1960s the ADA was paying to sponsor "The Adventures of Ozzie and Harriet," and the most reassuring family on television would turn to the camera between scenes and tell you to drink a glass of milk with every meal. Schools ran the same ritual, lining kids up in front of steel containers for a daily serving. Doctors told pregnant women to drink more for the baby. None of it happened by accident, and within two generations Americans had inherited a habit that a trade group engineered to empty a warehouse (Sentient Media; Milwaukee Magazine).

India reached a similar place by walking in from the opposite side, and that difference matters later. There was no surplus to unload. There was a shortage, and millions of small farmers getting squeezed by the traders who stood between them and the market. In 1946, in Anand, farmers set up the cooperative that became Amul, and Verghese Kurien, who joined in 1949, turned it into the engine behind Operation Flood, the largest dairy development program the world has ever run, and it turned a milk-deficient country into the biggest milk producer on the planet (World Food Prize; Amul Dairy). Amul didn't have to invent the desire, since dairy was already woven into Indian cooking. What it did was industrialize that desire, put a brand on it, and land packaged milk, butter and curd in hundreds of millions of homes that had never bought them in a carton.

The mechanisms were opposite, and the outcome was the same. One country conjured demand to move a surplus, the other organized demand that already existed until it was everywhere, and in both cases something outside the body decided what belonged on the plate, then held there long enough that it felt like it had always been there. Hold onto that, because for the first time in 100 years we've built a technology that runs the whole machine backward.

The un-manufacturing.

GLP-1 drugs (Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound) don't sell you a thing to eat or drink. They sell you the disappearance of wanting it, and the consumer economy has never had to reckon with a product like that. The uptake looks nothing like the slow burn of a trend working its way through one corner of a market. Roughly one in eight American adults is on a GLP-1 today, and about 18% have taken one at some point (KFF, November 2025). By May 2026, PwC's household data had a current user living in one of every five American homes, up from 9% at the start of 2025. When a fifth of the country's households change how much they eat inside 18 months, you're not looking at a niche behavior anymore; you're looking at the demand side of the entire food economy moving at once.

One clarification before this goes further, because these conversations tend to collapse into a fight I don't mean to start. I'm not talking about the medical case, and I want it walled off. If you're managing type 2 diabetes, obesity, or the cardiovascular risk these drugs now carry an indication for, none of my argument is pointed at you. The drug is doing work your body can't do alone and it's worth what it costs. I'm not against medicine and I'm not against people trying something new. What I'm circling is narrower and it's psychological. It's the far larger group reaching for a prescription as a shortcut around the effort, and what they're signing up for when they do.

Rented restraint and owned restraint.

The distinction I keep coming back to is ownership. Restraint you build for yourself is holistic, and it belongs to you. You changed what you eat and how you move, the habit rewired itself, and it holds even on the nights you're not thinking about it. Restraint that comes out of a pen is real too, but you're renting it, and it stays only as long as the payments do.

We know it's a rental because the trial data leaves no room to argue. In the STEP 1 extension, published in Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism in 2022, patients on semaglutide lost 17.3% of their body weight across 68 weeks. Then the researchers took the drug away. A year later, two-thirds of the lost weight had come back (Wilding et al., 2022). Nothing about the body had learned a thing. It had been overruled, and the moment the ruling lifted, the old appetite walked back in and reclaimed the ground.

That's the part I'd want anyone using this for looks or convenience to think about. The drug is a bridge, and I've got no problem with a bridge. But a bridge is only worth building if you plan to cross it and step off on the far side, and what the data describes is a crowd setting up camp in the middle with no intention of reaching the other bank. If the only thing holding the number down is a weekly injection, the result was never yours. You leased it, on terms nobody's reading closely, while the long-run side effects are still being worked out and all of us are early. My honest position is that if you can get there on food and movement, get there on food and movement, because that's the version the body keeps. The drug should be the thing that carries you to the exit, not the thing you stay on for a decade while the science catches up to what a decade of it does.

Now put a fifth of a country on rented restraint at the same moment, and the appetite that dairy and snack companies spent a century constructing doesn't just cool off. It reprices, right across the plate.

The meal comes apart.

Structure goes first. Breakfast, lunch, dinner, and the several snacking occasions the industry invented to fill the gaps between them, all of it was built on the assumption that hunger shows up on a timetable. GLP-1 erases the timetable. The reach between meals, the second serving, the thing you told yourself you'd earned by nine at night, those calories go first, because they were never hunger to begin with. They were habit and reward, and quieting reward is the one thing the drug does best.

Watch the restaurant that turned excess into its entire personality. The Cheesecake Factory built a brand on plates that clear 1,500 calories without trying, with a single pasta dish crossing 2,000 before the cheesecake even arrives. Abundance you couldn't finish was the whole promise. In March 2026, J.P. Morgan upgraded the stock, and the reasoning behind the upgrade is the giveaway: analysts figured the chain could keep its GLP-1 customers because of the Skinnylicious menu, the 500-calorie section it has kept quietly parked on the menu for years, which now gives a shrunken appetite a place to land (Fortune; J.P. Morgan). The upbeat case for the most maximalist restaurant in the country now rests on the small-plate section it used to ignore.

The math underneath is unkind even when the customer comes back through the door. A table that once ordered two full entrees, a milkshake, a starter and cheesecake now orders two Skinnylicious salads and a Diet Coke. Same table, same rent, same server, and a check worth a fraction of the old one. When the average spend per table drops, it drops straight to the bottom line, because none of the cost of serving that table went anywhere. Olive Garden and others are shrinking portions for the same reason (Fortune). The basic unit of the restaurant business, the occupied table, is worth less than it used to be, and hospitality can't fix a guest who isn't hungry.

The macros flip.

What people do still eat swings hard toward protein and away from refined carbs. Cornell and the market-intelligence firm Numerator tracked this through household purchase data: inside six months of a home starting a GLP-1, savory snack purchases dropped around 11%, while nutrient-dense buys like yogurt and fresh produce edged up (Food Dive; NJBIZ). Better food in the cart, less junk, right at the register.

I wrote about the same protein migration in India last year, where it's being pulled by a rising consumer class that finally has the income to want the good stuff. GLP-1 is the Western cut of the same story, except it arrives through a needle rather than an aspiration. Both roads run in the same direction, which means anyone selling protein, fiber and satiety is catching a tailwind from two unrelated engines at once. That doesn't happen often, and it's worth being long on.

What it does to a snack company's money.

When somebody starts one of these drugs, they eat less, and they don't cut across the board. What goes first is the chips, the soda, the cookies, the extra thing grabbed at the register. Those happen to be the most profitable items in the store. A bag of chips is a few cents of potato and oil selling for five dollars, and when it stays on the shelf the company doesn't lose an average sale, it loses its best one, the sale that was quietly paying for all the low-margin staples nobody makes money on.

Then it compounds, and this is the part that decides who makes it through. A snack factory is built to run full. The plant, the lines, the trucks, the payroll, you cover all of it whether the line runs at 100% or 95%. So a 5% drop in volume doesn't take 5% off your costs; it barely touches them, which means it doesn't take 5% off your profit either. It takes far more, because the same fixed cost is now carried by fewer bags. Operating leverage is a beautiful thing on the way up and a wrecking ball on the way down.

Here's the figure that should make a CFO put the coffee down. Cornell and Numerator found the average GLP-1 household trims its grocery bill by about 5.5%, roughly $416 a year, and nearer $690 for households earning over $125,000 (Food Dive). Run it across the map. There are about 132 million American households; put a current user in a fifth of them and call the average cut $500, and you're looking at north of $13 billion a year walking out of the American grocery basket. $13 billion is only about 1% of the $1.10 trillion Americans spend on groceries, but it lands almost entirely on the highest-margin corners of the store, which is why the profit hit runs far ahead of the sales hit. That's also more than the Hershey Company sells in a year, the whole of it, Reese's and Kisses and everything else, gone from the cart annually, and the number climbs with every new prescription written (Hershey FY2024). You can already read it in the incumbents. PepsiCo closed 2025 with organic volume and revenue down about 2% and operating profit off 7%, as North American shoppers pulled back from Frito-Lay, and it's now cutting prices on Lay's and Doritos and closing Frito-Lay plants to defend the business (Newsweek; Food Business News). Some of that is bargain-hunting shoppers. A growing piece of it is shoppers who just aren't hungry for the third bag.

So what do you do with this if you're building or investing? If you're the incumbent, the volume-heavy, high-fixed-cost snack business is the most exposed thing you own, and the honest response is to reformulate toward protein and satiety, take pack sizes down before the market takes them down for you, and stop penciling the last decade's volume growth into the next decade's factories. If you're the startup, the open field is the companion economy: smaller portions built for a muted appetite, high-protein and high-fiber formats, muscle-preservation nutrition, supplements that ride alongside the drug instead of fighting it. And the same warning holds for everyone. Demand chained to a prescription can reverse as fast as it appeared, so build a balance sheet that survives the morning a slice of your growth turns out to have been rented too.

Alcohol, and the death of the treat.

Alcohol is getting caught in the same net, partly through a side effect nobody set out to create. In a randomized trial, patients on a GLP-1 were about 29% more likely to cut back on drinking, and broader reviews have found real drops in problem-drinking scores (dulaglutide RCT; Lancet eClinicalMedicine). In the UK, 39% of people on these drugs said they're drinking less (Drinkaware). The drug turns down the reward circuit, and a drink is a reward.

The bigger move is psychological, and it stacks on top of a shift that was already underway. I've argued before that being sober-curious turned into an identity for Gen Z rather than a sacrifice. Now drop a medication that makes the drink itself less appealing on top of a culture that already made skipping it aspirational. The whole "treat yourself" reflex, the emotional machinery that sold indulgence for a hundred years, gets quieter from both directions. When wanting the treat is chemically dialed down and celebrating restraint is the cool posture, the categories that sold themselves as small rewards have a demand problem no marketing budget can outspend.

Insurance, and the take I'll put my name on.

My prediction cuts against the room. Over time, GLP-1 makes health insurance cheaper, not more expensive.

Right now, it's doing the opposite, which is why most people stop reading their own logic too early. The drugs run about $766 a month net of rebates, they already eat roughly 20% of employer prescription spend, and that spend jumped about 50% in 2025 alone (Mercer; EBRI). Simulations put the premium hit from covering them at anywhere from 5.3% to 13.8%, which is why only about a quarter of employers cover them for weight loss at all, and some are already backing out (EBRI; Mercer). That pain is real and it's loud. It's also the near term, and the near term is not where this argument lives.

What an insurer is pricing is future medical risk, and this is where the room has the story backward. In the SELECT trial, 17,604 people with heart disease and obesity but no diabetes were put on semaglutide, and their rate of major cardiovascular events, the heart attacks, the strokes, the cardiovascular deaths, fell by 20% (SELECT, NEJM 2023). Those are the claims that break a risk pool. One heart attack, with the stent and the rehab behind it, costs a health plan far more than the years of a drug that made the heart attack less likely in the first place. Now set that against the size of the exposed group: roughly 42% of privately insured adults under 65 already qualify under current FDA labels (Peterson-KFF). This isn't a sliver of the pool getting healthier. It's close to half of it.

Run those two facts together and the arithmetic turns over. Fewer catastrophic claims eventually make for a cheaper pool, and a cheaper pool eventually makes for lower premiums. It takes years because of a timing mismatch: you pay the full drug cost today and collect the avoided heart attacks a decade out, and insurers are slow to re-rate a pool they haven't finished watching. Then the drug cost itself caves. India already sells the same molecule for about ₹1,290 a month now that the patent has fallen; the US branded shield holds until 2031, but when it breaks the biggest line item in this whole equation drops toward the floor, and the near-term premium drag goes with it. Cheaper drug, healthier pool, and lower risk. That's the case, and it's why the second country is about to run the same experiment on fast-forward.

India is the other lab, and the cheaper one.

Almost none of what I've described has reached India yet. It tends to run a few years behind the US on shifts like this, and that lag is usually where the smart money gets positioned, because you can watch the American tape and place your bet before the local version shows up. The early signal is already loud, though, and it's moving faster than the usual lag would predict. Eli Lilly launched Mounjaro in India in March 2025. By October it was the single best-selling drug in the country by value, ahead of the antibiotics that normally hold that spot, with about ₹233 crore (roughly $28M) in sales in its first six months (India Briefing; Business Today). India has an estimated 254 million people living with obesity, and its obesity-drug market is projected to grow from around ₹3,500 crore now toward ₹25,000 crore (roughly $3B) by 2030.

Then comes the thing that makes India a different experiment and not just a slower one, which is price. Semaglutide's patent lapsed in India on March 20, 2026, and Sun, Dr. Reddy's, Zydus, Lupin and others put generics on the market almost overnight. Prices are dropping by more than half, in places by as much as 90%, with one brand going for about ₹1,290 a month, close to $15 (Pharmacy Business). In the US the same molecule sits behind roughly $1,000-a-month pricing and an insurance gate, with patent protection stretching to 2031. In India it costs about what a household spends on tea.

That scrambles the usual order of adoption. Income normally decides who gets the new thing first, and in the US it still does, with usage skewing toward higher earners and the coasts. India could leapfrog on access precisely because the branded gate falls apart the second the generics arrive. And it lands at the most loaded moment imaginable. I've argued that India's rising middle class has finally got the money to want the real thing, the protein, the coffee, the branded indulgence its parents couldn't afford. Now a $15 injection shows up to tell that same newly comfortable consumer to want less, right as they earned the means to want more. Amul spent 70 years teaching a country to reach for packaged dairy. We're about to find out what happens when a cheap molecule starts trimming appetite the other way, in the one big market still climbing.

What stays.

Take all of it back to the start, and you land on the same split. Milk didn't last because it was true; it lasted because someone built the habit deep enough that it outlived the campaign that created it. The weight people drop on a rented drug doesn't last, because nothing was built underneath it, which is why two-thirds of it comes back inside a year.

A big share of the demand destruction the market is busy pricing as permanent is rented, and rented demand comes back. The companies treating a 5% volume decline as the new floor are going to get surprised twice, first by how fast it dropped and then by how much of it returns when a wave of lifestyle users decides the injection wasn't worth the decade. The durable version of all of this, the healthier population and the lighter cart and the cheaper risk pool, only holds for the people who treated the drug as a bridge and then did the boring work of food and movement once they were across.

You build it with what you eat and how you move, and that's the only version that stays yours. The rest is a lease, and the lease always comes due.
