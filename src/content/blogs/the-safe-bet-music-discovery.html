I DJ'd for the better part of a decade. I don't do much of it now. That time went into my health instead, which is an article for another day. Music stayed central to my life, and for a while something has bothered me: almost nothing I listen to is new. I assumed that was me getting older. Then I looked at the numbers.

In 2024, record labels put $8.1 billion into A&R and marketing, the highest figure on record and about 27% of total industry revenue. It bought less listening in the market that matters most. U.S. streams of music released in the previous 18 months fell 3.3% year on year in the first half of 2025, to 168.5 billion. Global streaming grew almost 10% that year, to 5.1 trillion plays. The industry is spending more than ever to find new artists and getting less return on it in the room that counts. This article is about that gap: what's causing it, who profits from it, and what I think happens next to Spotify, which leads this market and increasingly decides both what we hear and how we find it.

Sidebar: A&R stands for Artists and Repertoire. It's the part of a label that finds new artists, signs them, and shepherds the record. Think of it as the industry's research and development budget.

First, the story you've probably heard is wrong

For years the standard line was that streaming killed the music business. It didn't. Recorded music revenue grew 6.4% to $31.7 billion in 2025, the eleventh straight year of growth. Nobody starved this industry. What changed is what the money buys.

For decades a record label worked like a venture fund. Sign ten albums, two take off, and those two pay for the other eight plus the building. It was unfair to the eight who missed, and it was also the only model that reliably produced great new art, because you can't find a rare artist without paying for the misses along the way. That trade has stopped clearing. Two things broke it. Attention got harder to reach, because 253 million tracks are fighting over a fixed amount of listening. And capital started favoring the sure thing over the discovery, because a superfan who already loves an act is safer money than a stranger who might love a new one.

New music lost plays, not just share

In the first half of 2025, music older than 18 months took 75.8% of all U.S. streams. Fresh releases got 24.2%. People misread that number constantly, so let's deal with the two fair objections upfront. First, the catalog pool grows on its own. Whatever came out this morning becomes catalog in 18 months, so a rising catalog share is partly just arithmetic. Second, a lot of streaming's subscriber growth is now coming from Latin America, Africa, India and Southeast Asia, where listeners are working through deep local catalogs they couldn't easily reach before. Both points hold. Neither explains the harder number, though. New music didn't only lose share. It lost 3.3% of its actual plays, in the world's largest music market, in a year when global listening rose 10%.

There's also a catch buried in that $8.1 billion figure, and it's worth knowing before someone quotes it. IFPI reports A&R and marketing as a single blended line and publishes no split between them. So the number held up as proof that labels still back unknown artists also includes every dollar spent pushing acts that already work. How has that mix shifted over a decade? Nobody outside the majors knows, and nobody has been made to say.

There's more music than ever, and half of it isn't human

The lazy version of this argument is that nobody makes original music anymore. That isn't true. Supply has been exploding. By the end of 2025, streaming platforms held 253 million tracks, up nearly 38 million in a single year. That's roughly 106,000 new uploads every day, and over 96% of them arrive through DIY and independent distributors. Supply grew 18% in twelve months.

Deezer is the only major service publishing a breakdown, and it reports that most of the daily flood is now machine-made. AI-generated tracks hit 44% of daily uploads by April 2026, around 75,000 songs a day, up from 10,000 a day in early 2025. By that summer it crossed 50%. Spotify deleted 75 million AI spam tracks in a single sweep in late 2025. Almost nobody listens to any of it. AI tracks are 1% to 3% of Deezer streams, and 85% of those plays get flagged as bot fraud and demonetized. One number reorganised how I think about this. Of those 253 million tracks, more than 120 million got fewer than ten plays across the entire year. Not ten a day. Ten in total. Eighty-eight percent got under a thousand. Meanwhile roughly 0.2% of all tracks captured nearly half of all listening worldwide. Supply grew 18%. Human attention didn't, because human attention is finite. Uploading a track to Spotify costs about twenty dollars and an afternoon. The streaming industry sold that as democratization, and it was, until the volume broke the system. When distribution costs nothing, distribution is worth nothing. Everything the old industry charged for, pressing vinyl, shipping CDs, retail shelf space, radio promotion, collapsed into a commodity anyone can buy. What stayed scarce was being found. So the whole ecosystem shifted toward selling the one thing left that still has value: discovery.

Spotify's smartest business is hidden in plain sight

I use Spotify daily and I like the product. That's why I've spent so long working out how it makes money. If you work in retail or tech, you've seen this playbook before. Amazon sold goods at thin margins for years, then realised the real prize was placement on the search page and started charging its own suppliers for visibility. That sponsored ad business did $68.6 billion in 2025, making Amazon the third biggest digital ad platform behind Google and Meta. The store became the landlord, and landlording turned out to be the better business.

Spotify runs a version of the same play. It's called Discovery Mode. An artist or label opts a track in. Spotify then pushes it harder in algorithmic recommendations, radio and autoplay. In exchange, the artist accepts roughly 30% lower royalties on the plays that promotion generates. And no money changes hands.

Say a track would normally earn $1,000 from a batch of streams. Switch on Discovery Mode, the track gets pushed to more listeners, and it earns more plays. But on those plays the artist is paid about 30% less. Spotify never sends an invoice. It simply keeps more of what it would otherwise have paid out. Economically, that's an advertising fee. On the accounts, it looks like a lower cost. Which is why most takes on Spotify miss it. Look at the advertising line, €1.84 billion out of €17.19 billion in 2025, and you'd fairly conclude Spotify is a subscription company with a small ad business on the side.

But placement income never touches that line. Discovery Mode reportedly brought in over €60 million in its first year, back in 2021, and Spotify hasn't published a figure since. By booking paid placement as a reduction in royalty costs, Spotify grows a high-margin business that never appears on a slide, and a revenue stream nobody has to disclose is one nobody has to defend. By the second quarter of 2026, Spotify's gross margin hit a record 33.4%. I'm not going to claim Discovery Mode explains that number, because on the one figure Spotify ever published it's nowhere near big enough. My claim is narrower: the mechanism can compound for a decade and nobody outside the company will ever be able to check.

This is where the two halves of the industry's spending meet. Labels pay to find and develop an artist. Then, to get that artist heard over 106,000 new uploads a day, many pay Spotify again, not in cash but in royalty points, through Discovery Mode. The same dollar that used to fully fund a record now has to clear two tolls: the label's own bet on the artist, and the platform's fee for surfacing it. That second toll doesn't show up in the $8.1 billion IFPI publishes, because it isn't paid. It's kept.

The next dollar comes from the ticket, not the stream

If returns on new music keep compressing and subscription prices have a ceiling, you stop chasing more listeners and start monetising the ones who care most. Spotify says it has helped drive over $1.5 billion in ticket sales through its ticketing integrations. In 2025 alone, nearly 25% more artists used the feature to list tour dates than the year before. In 2026 it expanded in-app buying with SeatGeek and rolled out presales through Live Nation, reserved for an artist's top listeners.

The superfan saga makes the strategy obvious. There were rumours all through 2025 about a $5.99 a month "Music Pro" add-on. It never launched. Instead, DJ tools, lossless audio and presale access all went into standard Premium at no extra cost. Spotify chose to defend $12.99 rather than reach for $19. That only makes sense if the superfan money was never meant to come from the subscription at all. It comes from the ticket, and after that the merchandise.

This is my prediction: Netflix merges with Spotify

This belongs to everything above, not beside it. Spotify is the only company at the top of this market that has to earn its cost of capital on music, and that constraint is why Discovery Mode exists at all. So who ends up owning Spotify is a question about discovery, not just about corporate structure. I think Netflix merges with Spotify within three to five years. Let me explain why, and then why it might not happen.

Start with Spotify's position. It reached 300 million paid subscribers and 777 million monthly users, holding roughly 31% of the global paid market, more than Apple Music and Amazon Music combined. But it's alone in a way the others aren't. Apple, Amazon and Google all treat music as a retention feature for a bigger ecosystem. None of them needs music to turn a profit on its own. Spotify does, and it took 17 years from launch to reach about $2.5 billion in annual operating profit. Every aggressive move it makes, Discovery Mode included, comes from competing against three companies that don't need the money.

Netflix has both the appetite and the machinery for a deal this size. Earlier in 2026 it bid $83 billion for Warner Bros Discovery with financing committed. Warner took a superior offer from Paramount Skydance, Netflix declined to raise, and it walked away with a $2.8 billion break fee that Paramount paid on Warner's behalf. So Netflix will attempt something transformative, can fund it, and knows when to stop bidding.

It's already testing music. Netflix has streamed live global concerts, its ad business is scaling fast, and it co-distributes podcasts with Spotify, including jointly funding a nine-figure talent deal. Two companies co-underwriting talent looks less like a partnership and more like a rehearsal. Everyone else is blocked. Apple, Amazon and Google would each be buying a business they already run, straight into an antitrust review none of them wants. Netflix has the subscriber base, the live ambition and the ad engine, and no music service.

Two things could stop it

The first is control. Daniel Ek and Martin Lorentzon hold roughly 69% of Spotify's voting power through special shares the public can't buy, as of the end of 2025. There is no hostile bid, no activist campaign, no proxy fight that works here. Spotify can only be sold if Ek decides to sell it. The second is price. At a valuation near $100 billion before any premium, this isn't an acquisition. It's a merger of near-equals, and it would rank among the largest media deals ever attempted.

But the corporate signals point one way. In January 2026, Ek moved to Executive Chairman, handing daily operations to two co-CEOs so he could focus on capital allocation and long-term strategy. That's not the job of someone running a company. It's the job of someone deciding what it becomes. After 17 years of compounding, subscriber growth slows and pricing hits a ceiling, and at some point choosing the right owner is the most valuable thing management can do. Lorentzon has already started, selling roughly $81 million of stock in recent months. The signal to watch is whether Ek follows him.

The risk got priced out, and nobody had to be a villain

If the deal happens, the last major platform whose economics depend entirely on music gets absorbed into an entertainment company that treats music as a feature for keeping subscribers.

And none of this required anybody to act badly. Labels are spending record amounts. Spotify is making rational margin decisions. The pension fund buying a catalog bond wants a coupon, not a discovery pipeline. The executive moving next quarter's budget toward the track that's already working is making the right call on the numbers in front of them. Add all those sensible decisions together and you get an industry that has quietly priced the risk out of finding new art. Which brings me back to where I started. I didn't get older. I got accurate. Almost nothing in this system is paid to help me find something new, so almost nothing new finds me.

The demand never went anywhere. There is still nothing quite like watching a room react to a song it has never heard. What's missing is anyone with a commercial reason to go looking for it.
